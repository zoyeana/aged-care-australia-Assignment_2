Ageing and aged care in Australia

Growing older: how Australia’s ageing population is reshaping aged care

Australians are living longer, and older people make up a growing share of every state and territory. This story follows that change from the population itself to the care system that supports it: who uses care, where it is delivered, and what it costs.

01 · An ageing nation

The oldest Australians are the fastest-growing group

Since 1980 the number of Australians aged 65 to 84 has more than tripled, from 1.3 million to 4.2 million.

The number aged 85 and over has grown even faster: six-fold, from about 99,000 to over 600,000. These are the people most likely to need aged care.

Because older age groups grew faster than younger ones, the whole shape of the population changed. People aged 65 and over were 9.6% of Australians in 1980 and 17.5% in 2025.

How to read a population pyramid: each bar is one five-year age group. Wider bars mean more people of that age. Move the slider forward and watch the top of the pyramid widen.

02 · Where Australia is oldest

Tasmania is the oldest state, and ageing fastest

Ageing is not happening evenly. In Tasmania, more than one in five residents is aged 65 or over. In the Northern Territory it is fewer than one in ten.

Comparing 2011 with 2025 shows how fast each state has changed. Every line slopes upwards: every state and territory now has a larger share of older residents than it did in 2011.

03 · How aged care is used

Most older Australians in care are supported at home

Aged care comes in three main forms:

  • Home care helps people keep living in their own homes.
  • Residential care provides a place to live with around-the-clock support.
  • Flexible care covers specialised programs, such as short-term restorative care after a hospital stay.

Home care is the most common: on 30 June 2025, 288,397 people received home care and 203,624 lived in residential care.

The people using care are mostly older women: about 65% of both home care and residential care recipients are women. Residential care also skews older than home care.

The largest states have the most people in care, mainly because more people live there. Switch between home care and residential care on the map to compare them on the same size ranges.

04 · Where care is delivered

Aged care homes cluster in the cities

Residential care depends on buildings, so where facilities are built shapes who can find care close to home. The map below places facilities at their real locations. They follow the population: dense along the east and south-east coast and around the capitals, sparse across inland and northern Australia.

Why it matters: older people in remote communities may need to move away from family and community to enter residential care.

05 · Home care needs

Most people at home receive mid-level support

Home Care Packages come in four levels, from Level 1 for basic support to Level 4 for people with high care needs.

Across most age groups, Levels 2 and 3 are the most common. Level 1 is rare at every age, at no more than about 5% of any age group.

06 · The cost of care

Spending has climbed every year

Supporting more older Australians costs more. After adjusting for inflation, combined government spending on residential, flexible and home care rose in every year from 2017–18 to 2024–25. The bars show spending each year; the line shows how fast it grew compared with the year before.

07 · Who needs the most help at home

High-level home care is most common at the youngest and oldest ages

Overall, 22.7% of Home Care Package recipients receive Level 4, the highest level of support.

The share is lowest among people in their 80s (about 20%) and rises sharply among the very old: almost half (46.8%) of recipients aged 100 or over are on Level 4.

Recipients under 65 are few, but a larger share of them are on Level 4 than people in their 70s and 80s.

08 · What it means

Planning care for an older Australia

The oldest group is growing fastest. Australians aged 85 and over have increased six-fold since 1980.

Every state is ageing, but not at the same pace. Tasmania is both the oldest state and the fastest-ageing.

Most care happens at home. Nearly six in ten reported aged care recipients are in home care.

Care homes follow the cities. Only 1.3% of residential facilities are in remote or very remote areas.

Costs are rising quickly. Combined spending grew by more than two-thirds between 2017–18 and 2024–25.

As the oldest age groups keep growing, deciding where care is provided, and making sure it reaches regional and remote communities, will matter as much as how much is spent.

About this page

Who it is for: everyday Australians who want to understand how an ageing population is changing their communities and what it means for aged care. No statistical knowledge is needed; terms are explained where they appear.

Why: to show where Australia is ageing fastest, how aged care is used and delivered, and how quickly its cost is growing, so that readers can see where pressure on care is building.

How: the page uses three map idioms (a choropleth, a proportional symbol map and a dot-density map) and further advanced idioms (a population pyramid, a slopegraph, a waffle chart, a butterfly chart and a heatmap), alongside a line chart, a bar-and-line chart and a lollipop chart. Interaction gives detail on demand (tooltips), comparison (care-type and state selectors) and change over time (the pyramid’s year slider).
